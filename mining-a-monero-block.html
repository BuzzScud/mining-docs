00

Prerequisite

A synced node, or someone else's

You cannot build on a tip you do not know. monerod gives you three things nothing else can: the current tip hash, the mempool you will choose transactions from, and the difficulty you must beat. Pool miners outsource this entire stage — which is also the moment they hand over the decision of which transactions get confirmed.

01

Stage one

Request a block template

The miner calls get_block_template. What comes back is a nearly-complete block with a hole in it:

  • prev_id — the hash of the current tip. Commit to the wrong one and you are mining an orphan.
  • Height, difficulty, expected reward — the terms of the bet.
  • Transactions from the mempool, ordered by fee per byte.
  • reserved_offset — a byte position inside the coinbase where you may write an extra nonce. This is the escape hatch for when the real nonce runs out.
  • The RandomX seed hash for the current epoch.

You will pull a fresh template every 20–30 seconds regardless, because new fee-paying transactions keep arriving and the tip keeps moving.

02

Stage two

Write yourself a coinbase

You construct a transaction with no inputs — money from nowhere, which the consensus rules permit exactly once per block, in exactly one amount:

reward = 0.6 XMR + Σ fees

Monero's emission curve ended in May 2022 at block 2,641,623. Since then the base reward has been a flat 0.6 XMR forever — the tail emission. It is a deliberate design choice: security should never have to rest on a fee market alone, so the subsidy never reaches zero.

Two rules constrain how greedy you can be:

  • Block weight penalty. Exceed the median weight of the last 100,000 blocks (floor: 300 kB) and your reward is cut quadratically. You may grow past the median only when the extra fees beat the penalty. Past 2× the median the block is simply invalid.
  • The 60-block lock. Your coinbase output cannot be spent for 60 blocks — about two hours.

The output itself is a one-time stealth address, indistinguishable on-chain from any other Monero output. Even the miner's reward is private.

02 / deeperemission · penalty · dynamic block size

Where 0.6 XMR came from

For twelve years Monero paid a smooth exponential curve with no halvings at all. Each block paid a fixed fraction of whatever supply remained:

MoneySupply = 2⁶⁴ − 1 atomic units (~18.446 M XMR) BaseReward = (MoneySupply − AlreadyGenerated) >> 20

A right-shift of 20 is a division by 1,048,576 — every block paid roughly one millionth of the remaining supply. No cliffs, no halving shocks to miner revenue, just a curve decaying toward zero.

It never reached zero. At block 2,641,623 on 30 May 2022 that formula's output fell below 0.6 XMR, and the tail emission took over as a hard floor. About 18.132 million XMR had been mined by then.

What the tail actually costs

262,800 blocks/year × 0.6 XMR = 157,680 XMR/year

At the switchover that was roughly 0.87% annual inflation. The numerator is fixed and the denominator grows forever, so the rate decays hyperbolically — under 0.8% by 2026, heading toward zero without ever arriving. Nominal supply is unbounded; the inflation rate is not.

The argument for it is security-budget continuity. Bitcoin's subsidy goes to zero, at which point fees alone must pay for all hashrate — an untested transition with a known failure mode, where fee volatility drives hashrate volatility drives reorg risk. Monero declined to run that experiment. A secondary benefit: the tail roughly offsets coins lost to forgotten keys.

The penalty, worked through

Monero has no fixed block size limit. Blocks may exceed the median, and the miner pays for the privilege out of their own reward:

Penalty = BaseReward × (B/M − 1)² for M < B ≤ 2M Reward = BaseReward − Penalty B > 2M → block invalid

When adding a transaction loses you money

Because the penalty is quadratic, the marginal cost of one more byte is linear in how far past the median you already sit:

d(Penalty)/dB = 2 × BaseReward × (B/M − 1) / M

At exactly the median that derivative is zero — the first bytes past M are free. The break-even fee a transaction must clear rises from there:

A rational miner includes a transaction only while its fee exceeds the penalty its bytes cause. That is the entire fee market: no hard cap, no permanent congestion, but sustained demand has to pay to push the median up.

Two medians, because one was attackable

A single median over a short window is a spam vector: rent hashrate, stuff blocks with your own junk, and the median ratchets up faster than honest demand ever would — permanently inflating everyone's storage cost. The 2019 rules made M a composite:

long_term_median = median of last 100,000 block weights (floor 300,000 B) short_term_median = median of last 100 block weights M = min( max(short_term_median, long_term_median), 50 × long_term_median )
  • The long-term median moves slowly, bounded to roughly 1.4× growth per 100,000-block period. It is the real ratchet, and it is deliberately hard to turn.
  • The short-term median lets genuine demand spikes through immediately, so a busy hour does not stall.
  • The 50× clamp bounds the burst. An attacker can force big blocks briefly and pay full penalty on every one, but cannot convert that spending into permanent chain bloat.

Weight is not size

Since Bulletproofs, a transaction's weight can exceed its byte size. Range proofs are logarithmic in output count, so a 16-output transaction is far smaller per output than a 2-output one — but not proportionally cheaper to verify. Left alone, that mismatch would let someone buy cheap verification load. Monero adds a clawback to the weight of transactions with more than two outputs, pricing them by the CPU time they impose rather than the bytes they occupy.

03

Stage three

Fold everything into 76 bytes

Hash every transaction — coinbase first — and build Monero's tree hash over those hashes to get a single root. Now assemble the object you will actually hash billions of times. It is small on purpose.

The timestamp is not free either: it must exceed the median of the last 60 blocks and sit no more than two hours ahead of network time.

04

Stage four

Build the RandomX dataset

RandomX does not hash the blob directly. First it needs a large block of memory derived from a seed that the whole network agrees on.

The seed is the hash of a block roughly 2048 back, rotating every 2048 blocks (~2.8 days) with a 64-block lag so every node switches at the same moment. Miners precompute the next epoch's dataset in advance so they never stall at the boundary.

05

Stage five

Run a program you have never seen

Here is where RandomX stops resembling a hash function. For each nonce:

  • Seed the VM. Blake2b the blob to initialize registers and a 2 MB scratchpad.
  • Generate a program. Roughly 256 instructions — integer arithmetic, floating point, branches, memory loads — synthesized from the current state.
  • Execute 8 loops, each 2048 iterations against the scratchpad, pulling random reads from the 2 GB dataset. Every loop generates the next program from the previous one's output.
  • Finalize. AES-hash the scratchpad, Blake2b the registers, emit 32 bytes.
Why this kills ASICs. To run this quickly you need branch prediction, out-of-order execution, a floating-point unit, large caches and 2 GB of low-latency memory. Build silicon that does all of it and you have built a CPU — a worse one, at far greater expense. Monero has hard-forked its algorithm several times specifically to keep this true.

It is also why hashrate here tracks L3 cache per thread (about 2 MB each) and memory latency far more than clock speed, and why huge pages are worth tens of percent.

05 / deeperthe virtual machine, instruction by instruction

The machine you are emulating

RandomX defines a small virtual CPU, and the design brief was explicit: it should resemble a modern x86-64 or ARM64 core as closely as possible, so that the best hardware for running it is a core that already exists.

  • 8 integer registers r0–r7, 64-bit.
  • 4 additive float registers f0–f3 and 4 multiplicative e0–e3, each 128 bits holding two IEEE-754 doubles. The e registers are constrained positive so multiplication chains never collapse to zero, infinity, or NaN.
  • 4 constant registers a0–a3, read-only for the program's lifetime.
  • A 2 MB scratchpad addressed in three tiers that deliberately mirror a real cache hierarchy: L1 = 16 KB, L2 = 256 KB, L3 = 2 MB. Most accesses land in L1/L2 so they hit actual silicon cache; the L3-wide accesses at loop boundaries are what make the full 2 MB resident and unavoidable.

The instruction mix is a hardware requirements document

Each program is exactly 256 instructions drawn from a fixed frequency distribution. The weights are not arbitrary — each exists to make some piece of a general-purpose CPU mandatory.

That last row is worth pausing on. One instruction in 256 changes the FPU's rounding mode mid-program, and every subsequent float result depends on it. A hardware designer therefore cannot hardwire round-to-nearest, cannot reorder float operations across it, and cannot substitute reduced-precision arithmetic anywhere. One instruction, and a whole class of shortcuts is gone.

One iteration

The loop body runs 2,048 times per program, and there are 8 programs. Each pass:

  • Derive two scratchpad addresses from the ma / mx memory registers.
  • XOR 8 integers from the scratchpad into r0–r7; load and transform 8 doubles into the f and e registers.
  • Execute all 256 instructions.
  • Mix mx with two register values, then read a 64-byte dataset item at ma and XOR it into the integer registers. This is the random 2 GB access — one per iteration, unpredictable, unprefetchable.
  • Swap ma and mx; write the registers back to the scratchpad.
256 instructions × 2,048 iterations × 8 programs = 4,194,304 instructions per hash + 16,384 random 64-byte reads across a 2 GB working set

At the end, AES-hash the entire 2 MB scratchpad down to 64 bytes and Blake2b that together with the final register file to produce the 32-byte result.

Why eight programs and not one

Each program is generated from the output of the previous one. You cannot know program 4 until program 3 has finished running. This chaining defeats the most obvious ASIC strategy — synthesize a circuit specialized to the program you are about to run — because by the time you know the program, running it is the only work left. Eight rounds is enough that per-program setup is amortized while precomputation stays impossible.

The dataset is tuned, not merely big

Dataset items are generated from the 256 MB cache by SuperscalarHash, a synthesized instruction sequence with a deliberately long dependency chain — roughly the latency of a DRAM access. That number is not incidental. It means a hypothetical cache-only miner that skipped the 2 GB expansion and computed items on demand would gain nothing: recomputing costs about what fetching costs. The memory-versus-compute tradeoff that normally lets hardware designers escape a memory-hard function is priced out of existence.

The same property is what makes light mode viable for verifiers. Computing a handful of items on the fly for one hash is cheap. Doing it 16,384 times per hash, billions of times over, is not.

What this means at your terminal

L3 cache is the real core count
Each thread needs its own 2 MB scratchpad resident in cache. A 16-core chip with 32 MB of L3 scales to 16 threads; one with 16 MB does not, and will hash faster on 8 threads than on 16. Count cache, not cores.
Huge pages are not optional
16,384 random reads per hash across 2 GB will thrash a 4 kB-page TLB relentlessly. 2 MB pages for the scratchpad and 1 GB pages for the dataset routinely recover 20–50%. This is the most common reason a machine hashes at half its potential.
Prefetchers actively hurt
The access pattern is random by construction, so hardware prefetch fills cache with data you will never use. XMRig's MSR tweaks retune this, and on Zen the gain is solidly double-digit.
Latency beats clock speed
The workload is memory-latency-bound more than compute-bound. Tightening RAM timings shows up directly in hashrate; a large core-clock bump often does not, while costing real watts.

The conclusion from the RandomX design notes: a dedicated ASIC might reach roughly 2× the efficiency of a general-purpose CPU. For SHA-256 the equivalent figure is on the order of 10,000×. That gap, not any promise, is the ASIC resistance.

06

Stage six

Compare against the target

target = 2²⁵⁶ / difficulty valid = int(hash, little-endian) < target

That is the entire game. There is no partial credit, no progress, no state carried between attempts. Each hash is an independent draw with probability 1/difficulty, and the process is memoryless — a miner is never "due."

Difficulty is what keeps the interval honest. Monero retargets every single block, over a 720-block window with the top and bottom 60 outliers discarded, aiming for 120 seconds. Trimming the outliers is what makes timestamp manipulation unprofitable.

06 / deeperretargeting · timewarp defense · the variance nobody warns you about

The algorithm in full

Monero retargets every block — no epochs, no 2016-block windows. To compute the next difficulty:

  • Take the last 720 blocks' timestamps and cumulative difficulties (about 24 hours).
  • Sort the timestamps. They are not necessarily in order — consensus bounds them only loosely.
  • Discard 60 from each end, leaving the middle 600.
  • timespan = last remaining timestamp − first remaining, clamped to at least 1.
  • total_work = sum of difficulty across the window.
D_next = ceil( total_work × 120 / timespan )

Read plainly: how much work did the network prove, over how much time, scaled to a 120-second block. Retargeting every block means hashrate that arrives or departs is absorbed within hours rather than weeks.

Why sort, and why cut exactly 60

Timestamps are miner-supplied, and the consensus constraints are loose: a block's timestamp must exceed the median of the last 60 blocks and must not sit far in the future. Within that slack, a miner can lie.

The lie that pays is the timewarp: report timestamps far ahead so the measured timespan looks enormous, the network appears slow, and difficulty collapses — then mine the cheap blocks. Sorting plus trimming is the defense, and the arithmetic is the whole point:

60 / 720 = 8.33% trimmed from each tail
  • Any timestamp in the outer 8.33% at either end is discarded before it is measured. A lone liar contributes nothing at all.
  • To move a trimmed boundary, an attacker must place more than 60 blocks in the 720-block window — over 8.33% of hashrate — and even then only shifts the endpoint to the next honest value.
  • Sorting first means a manipulated timestamp cannot poison the ordering, only its own position within it.

The cost is responsiveness: you are measuring the middle 600 of 720, so genuine hashrate shocks register slightly later. Monero took that trade deliberately.

From difficulty to target

target = 2²⁵⁶ / difficulty valid = int_LE(hash) < target

In practice monerod never performs that division. It checks whether the 256-bit hash multiplied by the difficulty overflows 2²⁵⁶, using a 128-bit high multiply — mathematically the same comparison, with no big-integer division and no rounding edge cases. Cheap enough to run on every block from every peer.

Note also that fork resolution uses cumulative difficulty, not block count. A longer chain of easy blocks loses to a shorter chain of hard ones.

07

Stage seven

Submit, and be checked by strangers

submit_block hands the full block to your node, which relays it. Every node that receives it re-derives the answer independently:

  • The RandomX hash is below target — computed in light mode from the 256 MB cache.
  • The difficulty matches what the chain expects at that height.
  • Timestamp is inside the median and future-time bounds.
  • Block weight and the penalty arithmetic are correct, and the reward is exactly right — not approximately.
  • Every transaction verifies: ring signatures, Bulletproofs+ range proofs, and critically no key image already spent. That last check is how Monero catches double-spends without ever seeing an amount or an address.

None of this requires trusting you. That is the point of making verification cheap.

07 / deeperwhat a node checks, and why none of it leaks

Header and coinbase

  • prev_id names a block this node already has, at the expected height.
  • The RandomX hash, recomputed in light mode from the 256 MB cache, is below target.
  • The difficulty matches what this node independently derived from its own 720-block window.
  • The timestamp exceeds the median of the last 60 blocks and is not far in the future.
  • Block weight is within 2× the effective median, and the penalty arithmetic is exact.
  • The coinbase has exactly one input of type txin_gen carrying the correct height, an unlock time of height + 60, and outputs summing to precisely the penalised reward plus fees. Not approximately — a single atomic unit over and the block is rejected.

Coinbase amounts are the one deliberate exception to Monero's privacy: they are plaintext, because everyone must be able to audit the emission schedule.

Ring signatures: CLSAG

Every input is signed with a CLSAG signature (Concise Linkable Spontaneous Anonymous Group), which replaced MLSAG in October 2020 at roughly 25% smaller and 10% faster to verify. One CLSAG proves two things at once:

  • The signer holds the private key for one of the ring's public keys — without revealing which.
  • The amount commitment for that same member balances — binding key and amount into a single proof rather than two.

Ring size is fixed at 16, raised from 11 in August 2022. Fixed, not minimum: a wallet choosing a larger ring would stand out, and a transaction that stands out has already lost. Uniformity is itself the privacy property.

Decoy selection is where privacy is won or lost

The ring's other 15 members are decoys pulled from prior outputs on chain, and how they are chosen is critical. Pick uniformly at random and you have destroyed the anonymity outright: real spends are overwhelmingly recent, so the newest ring member would be the true one nearly every time.

Monero's wallet therefore samples decoys from a gamma distribution over output age, fitted to the empirically observed distribution of real spend ages. The decoys are drawn from the same statistical population as the genuine input, so age alone tells an observer nothing. Outputs must also be at least 10 blocks old to be spendable, which closes a timing side-channel. And because every RingCT output carries a hidden amount, the whole output set is one undifferentiated pool — there are no denomination classes to partition it by.

Key images: stopping double-spends without identity

This is the mechanism that makes the entire design possible. For a one-time output key P with private key x, the spender must publish:

I = x · H_p(P) H_p = hash-to-point

Three properties do all the work:

  • Deterministic. That output can only ever produce that one key image, so spending it twice produces a collision.
  • Unlinkable. Because H_p maps to a curve point through a hash, I cannot be walked back to P. Nobody can tell which ring member it belongs to — or even which outputs on the chain are now spent.
  • Verifiable. The CLSAG proves I was formed correctly from some member of the ring.

Nodes keep a growing set of every key image ever seen; a duplicate is an instant reject. It is the one piece of unbounded consensus state Monero carries, and it is the price of spending privately.

Amounts: commitments plus Bulletproofs+

Amounts hide behind Pedersen commitments, C = aG + bH, where b is the amount and a a random blinding factor. Commitments are additively homomorphic, so a node verifies conservation of value without learning a single figure:

Σ(inputs) − Σ(outputs) − fee = 0

If that sums to zero, no coins were created. The fee stays plaintext, since miners must be able to compare it.

But commitments alone permit a fatal trick: commit to a negative amount — equivalently one that wraps past 2⁶⁴ — and the equation still balances while minting currency from nothing. So each output also carries a Bulletproofs+ range proof that its amount lies in [0, 2⁶⁴), with no trusted setup, logarithmic in size, and aggregated across the transaction's outputs. Bulletproofs+ arrived in August 2022, roughly 5–7% smaller and 15% faster to verify than the original Bulletproofs, which had themselves cut proof sizes by about 80% in 2018. Verification is batched across the whole block, which is what keeps a full node's cost tolerable.

What a validator learns, and what it never does

Public to every node

  • That the block's proof of work is valid
  • That every transaction is internally sound
  • That no key image has been spent before
  • That inputs and outputs balance exactly
  • Each transaction's fee, and the coinbase amount
  • The ring members — all 16 candidates per input

Never revealed

  • Which ring member was actually spent
  • The amount of any non-coinbase output
  • The sender's address
  • The recipient's address — outputs go to one-time stealth keys only the receiver can detect, using their view key
  • Whether any two transactions involve the same party

A node validates the chain completely while learning essentially nothing about who paid whom. That separation — full verifiability, zero disclosure — is why all the preceding machinery is worth its cost.

08

Stage eight

Win the propagation race

The block floods the network. If another miner found one at the same height moments earlier, the chain with more cumulative difficulty wins and the loser's block becomes an orphan — full work performed, zero reward. At 120-second blocks with healthy propagation this is rare, but never zero, and it is the reason miners keep their nodes well-connected.

Sixty blocks later, your coinbase unlocks. That is the first moment the reward is actually yours to spend.

What actually happens to you

Solo, your expected time to a block is network_hashrate ÷ your_hashrate × 2 min. At 10 kH/s against a multi-gigahash network, that is centuries. So almost nobody runs the pipeline above alone — the question becomes who runs stages 00 through 03 on your behalf, and that turns out to matter more than the payout mechanics.

Traditional

The operator runs the pipeline

You submit shares against an easy target; the pool builds templates, finds blocks, holds the reward and pays you proportionally minus a fee. Simple, and it concentrates transaction selection in a handful of hands.

Decentralized

You run the pipeline

P2Pool runs a sidechain of ~10-second blocks. When it produces a Monero-valid block, that block's own coinbase pays every recent share contributor directly. You run monerod, so stages 00–03 stay yours.

The consequence

Why this closes the loop

Monero fights ASICs to stop hashpower concentrating. Letting a few pool operators build every template would reintroduce exactly that, one layer up. Decentralized templates are what makes the rest of the design mean anything.
